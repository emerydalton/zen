# Nāgārjuna in Context: An Integrated Reading of the Wikipedia Article and Its Linked Sources

Nāgārjuna is best understood as a South Indian Buddhist monk, probably active in the late second or early third century CE, whose verse treatise, the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (MMK), argues that nothing has *svabhāva* (intrinsic, independent existence).\[1\]\[2\]\[3\] For him that claim is the same as saying that everything arises dependently. Read against its links, the Wikipedia article is sound on that core. It is weaker in three places. It treats his dates and many of his attributed works as more settled than the evidence allows. It projects later commentarial categories (Prāsaṅgika, the Pure Land patriarchate) back onto him. And it gives too little space to the modern disagreement about whether his arguments work and what they mean.

## TL;DR

- **Who he was:** Almost everything about Nāgārjuna's life is legend layered onto a thin core. That core is a Mahāyāna monk in the Andhra/Deccan region, probably linked to a late Sātavāhana king. The conventional dates of c. 150–250 CE are what Ian Mabbett (1998) calls a "self-validating majority vote," not a proven date.\[4\] Only about six "reasoning" works are broadly accepted as his; Mabbett reports Buston's list of six "specifically philosophical works" as the MMK, *Śūnyatāsaptati*, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa*, *Vyavahārasiddhi* and *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā*. Many texts that later traditions rely on are disputed, including the *Da zhidu lun*, the *Daśabhūmikavibhāṣā*, the tantric and alchemical works, and arguably the *Suhṛllekha*.
- **What he argued:** Nāgārjuna takes apart, one case at a time, every attempt to find a foundation that exists by itself. His targets include causation, motion, the self, the means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*), and even nirvāṇa and the Buddha. His conclusion is that "dependent arising is emptiness" (MMK 24.18), and that emptiness is itself empty. He presents this as a middle way between eternalism and annihilationism, not as nihilism.
- **How to read him:** No interpretation has won. The live options are the semantic or anti-realist reading (Siderits, Garfield, Westerhoff), the earlier absolutist reading (Stcherbatsky, Murti), the skeptical or Pyrrhonist reading, Kalupahana's empiricist reading as a return to the early Buddha, the dialetheist reading (Priest, Garfield), and Richard Hayes's charge that the arguments rest on equivocation. The commentarial split between Buddhapālita/Candrakīrti and Bhāviveka, which Tibet later formalized as Prāsaṅgika versus Svātantrika, is the historical root of most of these disputes.

## 2. Historical context: a minority movement in a divided India

The article places Nāgārjuna in a politically divided India, between the Kushan empire in the north and the Sātavāhana kingdom in the Deccan. In this period Buddhism was already split into several *nikāya* (monastic) schools. The article follows Joseph Walser in describing early Mahāyāna as a small, largely "invisible" minority living inside mainstream monasteries before the fifth century. Early Perfection of Wisdom (*Prajñāpāramitā*) sūtras, such as the *Aṣṭasāhasrikā*, were already circulating.\[2\]

This framing matters for reading his philosophy. If Walser is right, Nāgārjuna wrote as a Mahāyānist who had to persuade non-Mahāyāna monks using arguments and scriptures they already accepted. That would explain two features of the MMK: it leans heavily on mainstream Āgama material such as the *Kātyāyana* sermon, and it almost never names Mahāyāna sūtras.

Reviewers of Walser's *Nāgārjuna in Context* (2005) are less sure. Richard Nance's H-Buddhism review (January 2006) calls the book "pioneering" and "rich and rewarding," but also "somewhat problematic," judging Walser's reading of key passages "somewhat strained." Others say his precise placement depends on thin evidence, such as one *Ratnāvalī* reference to Buddha images seated on lotuses, matched against art-historical distribution.\[5\] The article reports Walser's conclusions without these reservations.

## 3. Biography: the thin core and the thick legend

### 3.1 What can be said

Sources agree on a minimum: Nāgārjuna was a Buddhist monk, probably active in southern India. The SEP calls the 150–250 CE range "rarely disputed."\[1\]\[6\] Yet the Wikipedia article itself says historians "do not agree" and widens the range to the first to third centuries.\[2\] Ian Mabbett's study ("The Problem of the Historical Nāgārjuna Revisited," *JAOS* 118.3, 1998) is the most careful review of the evidence. He shows how far apart the proposals are:\[4\]

| Proposal | Who proposed it |
|---|---|
| AD 50–120 | Venkata Ramanan |
| c. 50–150, western India | Ichimura |
| 150–250 | Kalupahana, following Nakamura |
| c. 243–300, from Chinese evidence | Lamotte and May |

Mabbett concludes that the usual second-century placement is "a self-validating majority vote, or a median possibility, rather than a demonstrable probability." He suspects Nāgārjuna may have lived later.\[4\]

The strongest thread of evidence is a tradition linking Nāgārjuna to a Sātavāhana king.\[2\] Paramārtha (sixth century), Xuanzang and Bāṇa's *Harṣacarita* (seventh century), and Yijing all attest it. Yijing says the *Suhṛllekha* (Letter to a Friend) was addressed to a king whose title clearly renders "Sātavāhana."\[4\] The article follows Walser in naming Yajña Śrī Śātakarṇi (late second century) on the basis of evidence from Amarāvatī.\[2\] Mabbett agrees he is "the more favored candidate" but argues that Vijaya Śrī Sātakarṇi (early third century) deserves more consideration. In his scenario, Nāgārjuna begins as a royal protégé and lives on under the Ikṣvākus.\[4\]

Mabbett also weighs some later evidence:

- The tenth chapter of the *Laṅkāvatāra*, added between 443 and 513, prophesies a monk "nāgāhvaya" in "Vedalī" who will refute existence and non-existence. He calls this "weak evidence."\[4\]
- A Jaggayyapeta inscription names a disciple of a teacher Nāgārjuna. On paleographical grounds it has been dated to about 600 by Burgess and to 450–500 by Ramachandran, which would support a later Nāgārjuna only if it refers to the philosopher.
- A Kumārajīva-circle preface, quoted by Jizang, gives a birth date equivalent to about AD 243. Mabbett doubts that Chinese sources had Indian records reliable enough to support such precision.\[4\]

### 3.2 The legend

The article handles the hagiography well. The earliest layer comes from Kumārajīva's circle (344–413): prefaces by Huiyuan, Sengzhao and Sengrui. These present Nāgārjuna as the greatest bodhisattva since Śākyamuni, who revived a weakened Mahāyāna through debate and writing.\[2\]

Later Chinese and Tibetan texts add much more. They make him a Brahmin convert and have him retrieve the *Prajñāpāramitā* from the palace of the nāgas. They also make him a master of mantras and alchemy who could turn rock into gold,\[2\] and a teacher who lived for centuries.

The IEP adds a key point the article only implies. The retrieval-from-the-nāgas story was part of the Mahāyāna's effort to authenticate itself.\[7\] Sūtras known to be recent had to be presented as the Buddha's own advanced teaching, held back until people were ready for it. In that story Nāgārjuna is the person who brings the hidden teaching out.\[7\]

### 3.3 Places: Nālandā and Nāgārjunakoṇḍa

Two places in the legend do not survive scrutiny.

- **Nālandā.** Tibetan accounts say Nāgārjuna studied there. Walser notes that Nālandā was not a strong monastic centre until about 425. He also notes that Xuanzang and Yijing, who both studied his texts there, report no local stories about him.\[2\]
- **Nāgārjunakoṇḍa.** The article notes that the excavations found no evidence connecting the site to Nāgārjuna.\[2\] The linked Nāgārjunakoṇḍa page says he was "once believed, probably wrongly" to have developed the site.\[8\] Third- and fourth-century inscriptions call the city Vijayapuri, capital of the Ikṣvāku dynasty (c. 225–310). The name "Nāgārjunakoṇḍa" is medieval.\[2\]\[9\]\[10\]

There is an irony here. Mabbett still regards the Krishna valley (Amarāvatī–Nāgārjunakoṇḍa) as the most plausible setting, though only because "no others are better."\[4\] So the region may be right even though the place-name is a later attribution.

### 3.4 Many Nāgārjunas

The article and its links agree that the name covers several people:

- the Madhyamaka philosopher;
- a tantric "Nāgārjuna II," the author of Vajrayāna works (Lindtner's label);\[2\]
- an alchemist or *rasāyana* author;\[2\]
- a Jain figure who may have fed into the Buddhist legends (Walser).\[2\]

The linked metallurgist page dates the alchemist to the tenth century.\[4\] It also reports a 1984 manuscript study showing that the *Rasaratnākara*, often credited to "Nāgārjuna," is consistently attributed in the manuscripts to Nityanātha Siddha. The alchemist Nāgārjuna is instead connected with a *Rasendramaṅgala*.\[11\]

Mabbett offers a unifying account. A single historical author of the MMK stands at the origin. The later tantric, alchemical and medical Nāgārjunas are "multiform particular manifestations of a single legend."\[4\]

## 4. Works and authorship

### 4.1 The core corpus

The article reports the main scholarly lists faithfully. Read together, they show a stable core and a disputed edge.

- **SEP (Westerhoff), "largely uncontested":**
  - argumentative works: *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (448 verses in 27 chapters, per Kalupahana's edition), *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā*, *Śūnyatāsaptati*, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa*, *Ratnāvalī*;
  - hymns: *Catuḥstava*;
  - letter: *Suhṛllekha*.
- **Westerhoff (2009):** works mainly from six texts that "expound a single, coherent philosophical system."\[2\]
- **Burton:** accepts seven "least controversial" works and judges Lindtner "rather liberal."\[2\]
- **Lindtner (1982):** accepts thirteen works, including the *Bodhicittavivaraṇa*.\[4\] Paul Williams has shown that this text is later.\[2\]
- **IEP:** in practice, "Nāgārjuna" is defined as whoever wrote the MMK and the *Vigrahavyāvartanī*.\[7\]
- **Candrakīrti (per Ruegg):** an early list of eight works.\[2\]
- **Tibetan tradition:** Buston counts six works in the "yukti corpus" (reasoning works); per Mabbett (1998) these are the MMK, *Śūnyatāsaptati*, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa*, *Vyavahārasiddhi* and *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā*. Tāranātha counts five.

The scale of the problem is clear from the numbers: more than 22 attributions in the Chinese canon, and anywhere from 116 (Oxford Bibliographies) to more than 140 (the Wikipedia article) to about 180 (Shambhala's reader's guide) in the Tibetan canon, depending on the count.

### 4.2 The disputed texts that later traditions rely on

The most important tension in the article is that it leans on texts its own scholarship section treats as doubtful.

- **Da zhidu lun (T 1509).** The article notes Lamotte's doubts.\[2\]\[12\] As Mabbett (1998) puts it, Lamotte "originally accepted the tradition but changed his mind before he edited the third volume" of his *Traité* (1970), concluding that the author was a Sarvāstivādin from the northwest who converted to Mahāyāna and lived after the author of the MMK. Ruegg (1981) proposes collective authorship, possibly including Kumārajīva.\[13\] Lindtner classes the work as spurious.\[2\]\[14\] Ramanan, de Jong and Yinshun still defend Nāgārjuna's authorship.\[13\] The text survives only in Kumārajīva's Chinese and is unknown in India and Tibet.\[2\] Yet it shaped Chinese Madhyamaka.\[12\]
- **Daśabhūmikavibhāṣā (T 1521).** The article's long Pure Land section rests on this text and presents its "easy path" teaching as Nāgārjuna's own. But its works section places the text only among Lindtner's "perhaps authentic" works.\[2\] The *Encyclopedia of Religion* calls the attribution "doubtful."\[15\] Hirakawa (1957) argued that it and the *Da zhidu lun* cannot share one author.\[13\] Lamotte noted that Kumārajīva attributed it to Nāgārjuna "wrongly or rightly."\[16\]
- **Suhṛllekha.** The article presents its closing dedication to Amitābha as Nāgārjuna's own voice.\[2\] Mabbett notes that S. Dietz (1983) doubts Nāgārjuna wrote it.\[4\]
- **Ratnāvalī.** Walser's historical case rests on it,\[2\]\[5\] yet Vetter has questioned its authenticity on metrical and word-frequency grounds.\[4\]

The result is that the "religious Nāgārjuna" of Pure Land devotion and the "historical Nāgārjuna" of the reasoning works are only loosely connected. The article presents both without making clear how unevenly the evidence supports each.

### 4.3 The hymns

The SEP notes that the *Catuḥstava* describes ultimate truth in positive terms that come "close to the theory of Buddha-nature."\[6\] This partly explains why Tibetan "Great Madhyamaka" (Dolpopa) and East Asian schools could claim Nāgārjuna for more positive accounts of the ultimate. It also means that any reading which sees Nāgārjuna as purely negative has to set the hymns aside or explain them.

## 5. The philosophy

### 5.1 Svabhāva: what exactly is denied

Everything turns on what *svabhāva* means. Westerhoff, the source for both the SEP entry and the article's account, distinguishes several senses:\[17\]

- **Essence**, such as the heat of fire. This is *not* Nāgārjuna's target.\[6\]
- **Substance**: a primary existent (*dravyasat*) that is the end point of dependence relations. This *is* the main target.\[6\]
- **Absolute reality**: the true nature of things. Westerhoff treats this as a special case of essence. Emptiness is "essential" to things in the way heat is to fire.\[6\]\[18\]
- **The cognitive sense**: the automatic way the mind projects independent existence onto things, which is the source of craving and suffering.\[6\] This sense has the most practical importance.

The article reduces this to "substance" and leaves out the warning about essence. That simplification explains a common confusion. Nāgārjuna does not deny that fire is hot. He denies that fire, heat, or anything else exists independently of conditions and of the conceptual framework that picks it out.

The IEP raises a point the article omits, from a linked scholar. Hayes argues that Nāgārjuna's arguments trade on two senses of *svabhāva*: "identity" (what distinguishes a thing) and "causal independence" (existing by itself). In MMK 1.5, Hayes argues, the first half of the verse is true only on the independence reading and the second half only on the identity reading.\[19\] If so, the argument equivocates. The IEP lists this as one of the "recently debated exegetical questions."\[7\] Westerhoff's layered account of *svabhāva* can be read as a reply.

### 5.2 Dependent origination equals emptiness, and how new that is

MMK 24.18 says that whatever arises dependently is what is called emptiness, and that this is a dependent designation and the middle path.\[19\]\[20\] MMK 24.19 adds that nothing is not dependently arisen, so nothing is not empty.\[7\]

The IEP explains why this matters against the Abhidharma. The *dharmas* (basic elements) of Abhidharma could serve as ultimate explanations only if they were exceptions to universal dependence. Nāgārjuna allows no exception: dependence goes "all the way down."\[7\]

Was this new? The article sets the older view (Stcherbatsky, Murti: Nāgārjuna invented emptiness doctrine) against more recent work.\[2\] Shi Huifeng (*Journal of the Centre for Buddhist Studies, Sri Lanka* 11, 2013) shows that early and mainstream sources already sometimes used "emptiness" for the process of dependent arising and dependent cessation.\[21\] Yinshun, the founding figure of modern Chinese Madhyamaka studies, read Nāgārjuna as continuing the Āgamas' account of dependent arising.\[13\]\[22\]

The best way to put it: Nāgārjuna did not invent the equation. He made it universal and turned it against the Abhidharma's foundational elements.

### 5.3 Method: refutation, the tetralemma, and "no master argument"

There is no single proof of emptiness. The SEP explains why. If nothing has intrinsic nature, no argument can establish its conclusion independent of context and audience.\[23\]\[24\] Refutation therefore has to proceed case by case, one opponent's candidate substance at a time.\[6\]

The main tool is the *catuṣkoṭi* (tetralemma). For any claim it considers four positions: it is so, it is not so, both, and neither. Nāgārjuna rejects all four.\[2\]\[7\] MMK 1.1 does this for causation: nothing arises from itself, from another, from both, or without a cause.\[7\]

The article calls this the "prāsaṅgika method." That is anachronistic. The IEP and the Svātantrika–Prāsaṅgika page both stress that the label is a later Tibetan doxographic construction, and the Wikipedia Svātantrika page says the inventor of the distinction "is not known."\[7\]\[25\]

Modern logicians disagree about what the tetralemma is. Graham Priest (*The Fifth Corner of Four*, 2018) models it in a paraconsistent first-degree-entailment logic, in which a statement can be true, false, both, or neither.\[26\]\[27\]\[28\] Deguchi, Garfield and Priest read some Madhyamaka paradoxes as true contradictions (dialetheias).\[29\] Others resist this. Garfield himself reads the positive tetralemma in MMK 18.8 by adding qualifications ("conventionally real, ultimately unreal"), following Tsongkhapa.\[30\]\[31\] Hayes, as noted, sees no need for deviant logic, only for spotting fallacies.\[19\]

### 5.4 Causation (MMK 1)

The four-way analysis, with the SEP's reconstruction:

1. **From itself.** If the effect were already in the cause, production would be redundant. Self-causation also conflicts with causes coming before their effects.\[6\]
2. **From another.** If cause and effect were substantially distinct, there would be no dependence relation between them. Then anything could produce anything; Candrakīrti's example is a sprout from coals as easily as from a seed. Also, the cause depends at least notionally on the effect.\[6\]\[7\]
3. **From both.** The marble-and-sculptor model avoids pure identity and pure difference. But it still makes each element depend on the others, so none exists by itself.\[6\]
4. **From neither.** Causelessness would make the world's evident regularity, and our causal access to knowledge, impossible.\[6\]

Nāgārjuna's conclusion is that the shared premise is false: cause and effect existing with *svabhāva*. Causes and effects depend on each other both notionally and existentially. MMK 4.3 says a cause cannot exist without an effect.\[6\]\[32\]

The article borrows Westerhoff's sharpest point. Causal dependence alone does not prove complete emptiness, because the Abhidharma saw no conflict between being caused and having *svabhāva*. The decisive step is conceptual dependence: the "causal field" is assembled by our interests.\[2\]\[6\] The SEP adds that Siderits and Westerhoff are still debating whether this inference from "caused" to "empty" holds (Siderits 2013 and 2025; Westerhoff 2023).\[6\]

### 5.5 Motion, change, and time (MMK 2, 7, 19)

MMK 2 is often compared with Zeno. Siderits and O'Brien (1976) read it as an attack on certain views of space and time. Westerhoff sees two other aims:\[6\]

- **Property-absence:** there is no mover without motion, so the individual depends on its property.\[6\]
- **Property-duplication:** "the mover moves" would need two motions, one that makes it a mover and one by which it moves.\[6\]

The broader lesson is that the line between what constitutes a thing and what it merely has is drawn by our concerns, not found in the world. Because "moving" (*gati*) also means moving from life to life in rebirth, the chapter also shows that saṃsāra, the one who is reborn, and liberation lack *svabhāva*.\[6\]\[17\] The chapters on the conditioned (7) and on time (19) apply the same pattern to arising, abiding and ceasing, and to past, present and future.

### 5.6 Self (MMK 9, 17, 18)

Nāgārjuna largely follows the Buddha's not-self analysis but adds two arguments of his own:

- **Against inferring a bearer of properties (MMK 18).** One might argue that because there are properties of a self, there must be a self that bears them. But the distinction between constitutive and instantiated properties carries no ontological weight, so the inference fails.\[6\]
- **On self-knowledge (MMK 9).** A substantial self would have to be essentially the subject of experience, so it could never also be its object.\[6\]

What remains is a self-designating stream of events that misunderstands itself. MMK 17.31–32 compares the agent to a magical illusion that produces further illusions.\[18\] This lets him keep karmic responsibility without a substantial agent: inside the dream, the rules of the dream still apply.\[6\]

The SEP adds a point aimed at the Abhidharma. For Nāgārjuna there is no substantial base, even of aggregates, on which a non-substantial self could be built.\[6\] This is exactly where he goes further than Abhidharma reductionism.

### 5.7 Knowledge and the Nyāya (Vigrahavyāvartanī, Vaidalyaprakaraṇa)

The article's long Nyāya section is new, and parts of it are worded in an essayistic way. Its core matches the SEP. Nāgārjuna asks how any means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*) can be grounded:\[2\]\[6\]

- **By another *pramāṇa*:** this leads to an infinite regress.\[2\]
- **By itself:** perception would then be its own object, which conflicts with MMK 3.2–3, where seeing cannot see itself.\[2\]
- **By mutual establishment with its objects:** this is circular, and still gives neither side intrinsic status.\[2\]

The upshot is that instruments and objects establish each other conventionally, and neither exists with *svabhāva*.\[2\]

Two linked pages complicate the picture. First, the Nyāya Sūtras page gives the text's date only vaguely, from the sixth century BCE to the second century CE. It also contains a garbled claim that Nāgārjuna "explicitly states" Nyāya Sūtra 4.2.25 is aimed at Madhyamaka,\[33\]\[34\] which is an obvious anachronism. Bronkhorst ("Nāgārjuna and the Naiyāyikas," *Journal of Indian Philosophy* 13, 1985) offers a better account:\[4\] some Nyāya sūtras may predate and influence Nāgārjuna, while others react to him.\[35\]

Second, the article presents Vātsyāyana, Uddyotakara, Vācaspati Miśra and Jayanta as one continuous realist reply.\[2\] That is broadly fair. But it hides how much later these thinkers are, and the "no thesis" hyperlink in that section oddly points to the article on *nihilism*.

Hayes adds a historical counterpoint. Despite Nāgārjuna's critique of grounded knowledge, the Buddhist epistemology of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti dominated later Indian Buddhism "without any explicit attempt to answer" him.\[19\]

### 5.8 The "no thesis" verse (Vigrahavyāvartanī 29)

"If I had a thesis, that fault would be mine; but I have no thesis."\[36\] Westerhoff ("The No-Thesis View," in *Pointing at the Moon*, 2009) separates three readings:\[37\]\[38\]

- **Argumentational:** only refute by reductio; never assert.\[38\]
- **Transcendent:** an inexpressible reality lies beyond language.\[38\]
- **Semantic** (his own): Nāgārjuna makes no claim understood on realist assumptions. He does not say his words or their referents have *svabhāva*.\[38\]\[39\]

The Nyāya objection is that the denial of all theses is itself a thesis.\[2\] That objection lands only against a realist account of meaning. Candrakīrti made "no thesis" central to his method. Tsongkhapa limited it to theses held to exist intrinsically. Some recent scholars read VV 29 as a reductio of the opponent rather than Nāgārjuna's own position.\[40\]

### 5.9 The two truths (MMK 24.8–10)

The Buddha's teaching rests on two truths, conventional (*saṃvṛti*) and ultimate (*paramārtha*). The ultimate cannot be taught without the conventional, and nirvāṇa cannot be reached without the ultimate.\[2\]\[7\] The IEP explains what is at stake.

For the Abhidharma, the two truths were two sets of things. Conventional truths concern aggregates. Ultimate truths concern the *dharmas*, which do the real explanatory work.\[7\]

Madhyamaka holds that the ultimate set is empty. The result, in Siderits's phrase, is that "the ultimate truth is that there is no ultimate truth."\[7\]\[41\]\[42\] The world as conventionally described cannot be eliminated. The IEP argues that this is why Madhyamaka, and not the Abhidharma, avoids the charge of "nihilism": the Abhidharma's ultimate level finally replaces the suffering persons whom compassion is meant to serve.\[7\] For Nāgārjuna they remain.

The view faces a reflexive puzzle: what status does this claim have? Madhyamaka's answer is the emptiness of emptiness. Emptiness is a corrective, not a ground. It dissolves wrong views and then dissolves itself. Grasped wrongly, it is like "a serpent wrongly held."\[2\]\[7\]

### 5.10 Nirvāṇa, saṃsāra, and the Buddha (MMK 22, 25)

- **The Buddha.** Analyzed like any person, the Tathāgata is neither identical to the aggregates nor separate from them, and so is "devoid of intrinsic nature."\[2\]
- **Nirvāṇa.** It is neither an existent, nor a non-existent, nor both, nor neither. The question of the Buddha after death is one of the traditional "unanswered" questions.\[2\]
- **Saṃsāra and nirvāṇa.** There is "not even the finest gap" between their limits.\[2\]

A citation error in the article should be noted. It places the saṃsāra–nirvāṇa verses at "ch. 22, verses 19–20," yet introduces them as part of "this nirvana chapter."\[2\] The IEP and the MMK chapter list confirm that they are MMK 25.19–20.\[7\]\[43\]

The chapter ends with the verse about the blissful calming of conceptual proliferation (*prapañca*), and the claim that the Buddha taught no dharma to anyone.\[2\] Hayes argues that this framing, together with the opening and closing dedications (dispelling "all views," MMK 27.30), shows Nāgārjuna's aim was the classic Buddhist one of ending views that presuppose a fixed personal identity.\[19\]

### 5.11 Ethics and the path

The religious works (*Ratnāvalī* and *Suhṛllekha*, with the authorship caveats in section 4) use conventional Mahāyāna ethics. They distinguish a Dharma of "ascendance" (*abhyudaya*), which brings happiness and good rebirth through faith and merit, from a Dharma of the "highest good" (*naiḥśreyasa*), which brings liberation through wisdom. Of the two, "wisdom is foremost, yet faith is its prerequisite."\[2\]

Stepien (2024) calls this an "eirenic" ethics that is grounded in groundlessness. Emptiness removes the line between self and other, and virtuous action eventually unravels action itself.\[2\] The SEP's dream analogy supplies the link to metaphysics: illusion-bound beings must still act by the illusion's laws.\[6\]

## 6. Opponents and interlocutors

- **Sarvāstivāda–Vaibhāṣika Abhidharma.** This is the main Buddhist target.\[2\] Its *dharmas* are substances with intrinsic natures, and it holds that past, present and future all exist. The IEP frames all of Madhyamaka as "constitutively opposed" to Abhidharma two-truths realism.\[7\] Hayes's counterpoint is that Abhidharma "continued to flourish for centuries" without any visible effort to answer Nāgārjuna.\[19\]
- **Mahāsāṃghika affiliation.** Walser suggests Nāgārjuna lived among Mahāsāṃghika sub-schools (Pūrvaśaila, Aparaśaila, Caitika). The article rightly notes that his texts defend no doctrines specific to any sect.\[2\]
- **Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika.** These are the main non-Buddhist targets. Nyāya supplies the *pramāṇa* epistemology and the sixteen categories that the *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa* attacks. Vaiśeṣika supplies atoms and the substance–quality–inherence ontology.\[2\]\[6\]
- **Sāṃkhya.** Its view that the effect pre-exists in the cause is the classic case of "arising from itself" in MMK 1.1.\[7\]
- **The early discourses.** Nāgārjuna presents the *Kātyāyana* sermon (*Kaccānagotta Sutta*, SN 12.15), on avoiding the extremes of "is" and "is not," as his authority. Kalupahana takes this furthest, calling the whole MMK "a grand commentary" on that sūtra.\[2\]\[44\]\[45\] He also places Nāgārjuna in a line with Moggaliputta-Tissa as a reviver of the original teaching.\[2\]

## 7. The commentarial tradition and later schools

### 7.1 India

**Āryadeva**, traditionally Nāgārjuna's direct disciple, wrote the *Catuḥśataka* (Four Hundred Verses). Dharmapāla and Candrakīrti later read this text in different ways, and the IEP notes that this divergence is sometimes taken as the point where Madhyamaka and Yogācāra split.\[7\]

The first full MMK commentaries are the *Akutobhayā*, which Lindtner judges inauthentic, and Piṅgala's commentary preserved in Kumārajīva's *Zhong lun*.\[2\]

The decisive debate involves three commentators:

- **Buddhapālita** (fl. c. 500) simply drew out the absurd consequences of each rejected position.\[7\]
- **Bhāviveka** (c. 500–570) objected. He drew on Dignāga's logic and recast the arguments as formal, independent inferences qualified by "ultimately."\[7\]\[46\] The article notes that Bhāviveka was also the first to use the term "Madhyamaka."\[2\]
- **Candrakīrti** (c. 600–650) defended Buddhapālita in the *Prasannapadā*. This is the only Sanskrit MMK commentary to survive, and it preserves the Sanskrit root text.\[7\]

The later Svātantrika line runs through Jñānagarbha (who separated true from false convention), Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla. Śāntarakṣita's *Madhyamakālaṃkāra* combines a Yogācāra account of the conventional with a Madhyamaka account of the ultimate.\[7\]\[47\]\[48\]

The IEP's key historical point corrects any Tibet-centred story. **In India, Candrakīrti had little influence.** Bhāviveka, Dharmakīrti and Śāntarakṣita set the course.\[7\]

### 7.2 Tibet

Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla, both Svātantrikas, brought scholastic Madhyamaka to Tibet. Kamalaśīla is remembered for defending gradual awakening against the Chan view of sudden awakening at the Samye debate. Yet through Atiśa and the "second dissemination," Candrakīrti became the official authority.\[7\] The SEP calls Prāsaṅgika "the official philosophical position of Tibetan Buddhism."\[6\]

Tsongkhapa (1357–1419), founder of the Gelug, sharpened the Svātantrika–Prāsaṅgika divide into a difference in ontology. Svātantrikas, he argued, still accept that things are conventionally established by their own characteristics.\[25\]\[49\] He limited the "object of negation" to intrinsic existence, which leaves conventional existence intact. Ironically, he also combined Candrakīrti with Dharmakīrti's epistemology, which is a Svātantrika-style move.\[7\]

Gorampa (1429–1489), of the Sakya school, attacked this.\[7\]\[50\] On his view, conventional truths are themselves to be negated, and the tetralemma's purpose is to go beyond logic altogether.\[50\]\[51\] He argued that Tsongkhapa's view collapses the ultimate into the conventional.\[7\] Gelug institutions suppressed his works, and Mipham (1846–1912) later drew on them.\[50\] Dolpopa's "Great Madhyamaka" (*shentong*) took the positive line hinted at in the hymns.

Sakya and Nyingma authors generally treat the Svātantrika–Prāsaṅgika split as less important.\[52\] So the "Prāsaṅgika Nāgārjuna" that the SEP presents as Tibetan orthodoxy is mainly Gelug orthodoxy.

### 7.3 East Asia

East Asia received a different Nāgārjuna. Kumārajīva translated the "Three Treatises" in Chang'an in the early fifth century:\[22\]

- the *Zhong lun* (MMK with Piṅgala's commentary);\[22\]
- the *Shiermen lun* (Twelve Gate Treatise), attributed to Nāgārjuna but surviving in neither Sanskrit nor Tibetan;\[7\]
- Āryadeva's *Bai lun* (Hundred Treatise).\[22\]

These, sometimes with the *Da zhidu lun*, became the basis of the Sanlun school.\[12\]\[22\] Later Indian Madhyamaka (Candrakīrti, Śāntarakṣita) and epistemology (Dharmakīrti) were never translated into Chinese.\[7\]

Sengzhao (374–414) combined Madhyamaka with Daoist and Xuanxue (Mystery Learning) language. Jizang (549–623) systematized the two truths and treated emptiness as "medicine" for the "disease" of views. Sanlun reached Japan in 625 through the Korean monk Hyegwan, according to the *Nihon Shoki*; as Sanron it became one of the six Nara schools, and Oxford Reference dates the death of the last actual Sanron master to 1149. As an independent school it faded after Jizang, but its themes passed into Tiantai and Chan/Zen. Hsueh-li Cheng calls Zen "a practical application of Madhyamika thought."

The IEP adds that East Asian interpreters characteristically tried to harmonize Madhyamaka with Yogācāra\[7\] and Buddha-nature thought.\[7\] Yinshun later criticized that move as a corruption of Nāgārjuna.\[22\] Through the *Da zhidu lun* and the *Daśabhūmikavibhāṣā*, East Asia also made Nāgārjuna a patriarch of Pure Land, Esoteric Buddhism, Chan and Tiantai. The article's infobox reflects this, listing Pure Land and Tiantai as his "schools,"\[2\] even though the scholarship section undercuts the texts these claims rest on.

## 8. Modern interpretations

The article lists Kantian idealism, nihilism, relativism and pragmatism, then the analytic turn.\[2\] Read with its links, the field falls into several camps.

1. **Nihilist.** This is the oldest charge, made by ancient Indian opponents and by early Western scholars. The SEP gives two replies: ontological nihilism may not even be coherent, and Mādhyamikas explicitly reject both extremes.\[6\] The IEP turns the charge around and aims it at the Abhidharma.\[7\]
2. **Absolutist / Kantian.** Stcherbatsky and Murti (*The Central Philosophy of Buddhism*, 1955) treat the two truths as phenomenon and noumenon, and emptiness as an Absolute reached by intuition (*prajñā*).\[53\]\[54\]\[55\] Critics point out that this imports a ground into a philosophy built to deny grounds.\[56\] Westerhoff remarks that it may be more useful to put on a Nāgārjunian lens to read Kant than the reverse.\[54\]
3. **Skeptical / Pyrrhonist.** The IEP calls skepticism the most frequent modern label.\[7\] McEvilley and Kuzminski find close parallels with Sextus Empiricus, down to the shared fire-and-fuel example.\[57\]\[58\] McEvilley proposes Greek influence on India; Beckwith proposes Buddhist influence on Pyrrho.\[2\]\[59\] The article's Pyrrhonism paragraph is grammatically broken and reports these influence claims too readily. Specialists judge the direct historical link "inconclusive," and Siderits rejects the "skeptic" label for Nāgārjuna.\[60\]\[61\]
4. **Semantic / anti-realist.** This is Siderits, Garfield and Westerhoff, now dominant in analytic work. Emptiness is a claim about truth: there is no mind-independent way the world is.\[42\] The view is criticized, for example by Ferraro, as defective and unlikely as a reading of the texts.\[62\] Siderits and Garfield reply that this misreads the slogan's parameters.\[63\]
5. **Empiricist / pragmatist.** Kalupahana reads Nāgārjuna as a faithful restorer of the Buddha's empiricism, "not a Mahāyānist."\[44\] This sits poorly with the Mahāyāna texts Nāgārjuna clearly uses, as the article itself notes.
6. **Dialetheist / paraconsistent.** Priest, Garfield and Deguchi accept some true contradictions at the limits of thought.\[29\]
7. **Therapeutic / Wittgensteinian.** Emptiness talk works to dissolve conceptual proliferation (*prapañca*) rather than to state doctrine (noted by the IEP).\[7\]
8. **Logical-critical.** Hayes ("Nāgārjuna's Appeal," 1994) argues that the arguments are fallacious, with equivocation on *svabhāva* as the main case. On his view, this explains both why Indian Buddhists largely ignored Nāgārjuna and why modern readers, finding the conclusions counterintuitive, have credited him with "deviant logic."\[19\]
9. **Contextual-historical.** Walser reads the philosophy as a strategy of a minority Mahāyāna within mixed monasteries.\[64\]\[65\]

**Assessment.** The semantic/anti-realist reading fits the reasoning works best. It explains why emptiness is empty, why the conventional cannot be eliminated, and why "no thesis" is not self-refuting. It is also the reading the article adopts as "more traditional and standard."\[2\] But the hymns and the Tibetan and East Asian traditions show that Nāgārjuna's own corpus, as received, supports more positive readings. Hayes's challenge has not been answered so much as absorbed: Westerhoff's account of *svabhāva* is in effect the reply.

## 9. Comparison with Western philosophy and later legacy

- **Ancient.** The closest Western parallel is Pyrrhonism: suspension of judgement, peace of mind, and refutation of dogmatists on their own terms. Nāgārjuna's arguments about motion invite comparison with Zeno.\[6\]
- **Modern.** Kant (via Murti), Wittgenstein and poststructuralism (via therapeutic readings), and contemporary metaphysics of grounding. Westerhoff's *The Non-Existence of the Real World* (2020) connects Madhyamaka anti-foundationalism to debates about whether grounding must bottom out.\[2\]\[6\] Priest connects it to non-classical logic.
- **Physics.** Carlo Rovelli (*Helgoland*) credits Nāgārjuna with shaping his relational interpretation of quantum mechanics. Finkelstein developed a related "universal relativity."\[2\] These are cases of inspiration, not evidence that Nāgārjuna anticipated physics.

On how much Nāgārjuna mattered, the SEP and Hayes are in clear tension. The SEP says he shaped Indian debate "for a thousand years."\[6\] Hayes ("Nāgārjuna's Appeal," *Journal of Indian Philosophy* 22, 1994) argues that, aside from a few commentators who identified themselves as Mādhyamikas, Indian Buddhist intellectual life went on "almost as if Nāgārjuna had never existed." The two can be reconciled. Through Bhāviveka and Śāntarakṣita, Madhyamaka became one pole of late Indian Buddhist thought. And Brahmanical realists did sharpen their epistemology against it, as the article argues. But both his Abhidharma and his epistemological targets survived without answering him directly. His largest effect came after India, in Tibet and East Asia, and now in modern philosophy.

## 11. Recommendations for readers

- Treat the MMK, *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, *Śūnyatāsaptati*, *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā* and *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa* as the evidence for "Nāgārjuna's philosophy." Treat Pure Land, tantric and alchemical claims as evidence for his *reception*.
- Read MMK 1, 15, 18, 24 and 25 first, in two translations with different interpretive leanings, such as Siderits and Katsura (semantic) and Garfield (Tibetan/Gelug-inflected). Add Kalupahana for the early-Buddhist reading.
- Use Westerhoff's SEP entry and his *Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka* (2009) as the best single guides. Then read Hayes (1994) as the strongest critique and Mabbett (1998) and Walser (2005) for history.
- When you meet "Prāsaṅgika" or "Svātantrika," ask whether the claim concerns Indian commentators (a dispute about method) or Tibetan doxography (claimed ontological differences).

## Caveats

- Several primary sources were consulted secondhand. These include Lindtner, Ruegg, Hirakawa and Lamotte, reached through Mabbett and recent surveys by Nishino (2020) and Travagnin (2018).\[13\]\[14\]
- The Wikipedia article is a moving target. The Nyāya section in particular is recent and essayistic.
- Dates for Indian figures other than Nāgārjuna (Buddhapālita, Bhāviveka, Candrakīrti) are also approximate.
- Claims about Greek–Indian influence remain speculative, in both directions.
